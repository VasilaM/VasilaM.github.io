eternal passersby

him.
Almost every day after school let out in tenth grade, I’d make the fifteen minute walk to the bazaar in our tiny town with my first love. On some days, I’d buy bread for next morning’s milk tea. The throngs of people we weaved through were nothing but background noise occasionally getting in the way of our unending conversation. In the moments when we were briefly separated by the chaos, I’d let myself relish in imagining that we were on this errand together in a faraway future, and the bread was for us. Once I caught up to him I’d take his hand, we’d hail a marshrutka and keep chatting all the way to our shared home where our old cat was waiting.

This September we saw each other again for the second time in four years. Immediately, it was different from the last. Perhaps back then our ill-fated breakup a year before graduation and its messy aftermath was still too fresh in both our minds. It had scarred us in different ways, neither of which had quite yet healed despite us both having experienced new relationships since. An unfamiliar awkwardness hung in the air along with the low fog that day we spent walking all across Almaty. Though we were doing our best to be Mature and Cool Adults, we didn’t quite know how to be around each other. We were inexplicably tense, avoiding eye contact and making comments that didn’t land. We were like two coarse slabs of stone repeatedly being struck against one another that rather than starting a warm fire just chipped away at each other and produced the occasional frustrated spark.

I have no good explanation beyond the passage of time as to why when he showed up this time, at the posh brunch cafe where I was sitting engrossed in my laptop, with that face that’ll forever be etched into my mind and a new broad set of shoulders, I was instantly oozing with easy affection. We hugged then again shook hands as soon as we sat down. We marvelled at how many summers and winters had passed (a classic Russian phrase for you haven’t seen someone dear in a long time). We smiled and smiled.

As we spent the next three days hopping around various trendy spots in Dushanbe, at times working and at times pretending to, talking all the while, I felt some of the biggest feelings I’d felt in a long time. Maybe it was amplified by my jet lag or the fact that I was deep into one of the most heart-wrenching novels I’d read in a long time, but I was positively buzzing with emotion throughout that period. Wonder, joy, heartache, hope. All of it coursed through my blood, radiated from my pores.

In an expansive two-story cafe that had more teenage waiters milling about than patrons (almost certainly a money laundering scheme), while I was recounting some story, I looked over and noticed he was tearing up a paper napkin into tiny little pieces and rolling them into balls. I stopped mid-sentence with wide eyes to tell him that I’d been teased for this habit all throughout college and never seen anyone else do it. Laughing, I remarked that one of us must’ve picked this up from the other. That little quirk was so ingrained in me that I never could’ve guessed when or how it started, and now it was like I had a mirror held up to myself revealing things I thought I could never know. It also felt charming juxtaposed against all the other little things we were re-discovering about each other that seemed completely at odds. I was the unabashed eldest daughter, he was the timid youngest son. I asked waiters for cold non-sweet drink recommendations, he ordered hot black tea with lemon and extra sugar.

In an all-white coffee shop that was trying to be European sophistication and felt more corporate soul-suck, I slid over to his side of the table to help him pair a bluetooth keyboard I’d gifted him to his computer. After I finally figured it out, he told me about his plans to meet the girl he was talking to for dinner that night. I’d heard about her several times now, so it wasn’t a surprise. That, of course, didn’t stop the churning in my stomach and constriction in my chest as I looked for restaurants as alternatives to the place he initially suggested which she’d deemed ‘cringe’. He was antsy until the matter was settled upon about ten minutes of waiting, and we left the coffee shop. As we walked, I came up with something to do for the rest of the evening so that my outing felt less silly. At a three-way intersection, I tactfully told him to go ahead as I doubled back to the that brunch spot I told him I’d planned to spend the evening reading in. He said he’d text me after he was done and meet up with me if I was still out. I smiled and nodded, knowing I wouldn’t be.

I found a spot in a secluded corner by the restaurant’s kitchen. The evening passed with me trying to avoid the workers’ eyes as I took turns writing in my notebook, reading my devastating novel, and trying to wipe my tears as discreetly as possible. About an hour later, after getting too queasy to pick at my dinner any longer and too emotional to keep my crying subtle, I departed. About three hours later, I finished the novel, bawling just as I had been when I finally reached my apartment. I checked my phone to see a message from him, 9.36pm, asking if I had gone home yet. We exchanged a series of texts about our evenings, and I couldn’t get to sleep until much later. That night I wrote the letter I would give him the following day, conveying the warmth I’d felt during this reunion of ours, my gratitude for his presence in my life, and my excitement at the prospect of many more such rendezvous to come. Even though we may spend forever only getting glimpses through the windows of each other’s lives, and we may not be able to shoulder the heaviest of burdens for one another, when our paths do cross we can set aside those burdens for a moment and simply rest. Basking in that warm familiarity, that comfort only the body remembers. When he interrogated me under the pounding afternoon sun about its subject matter after I thrust it at him on the street, shading my eyes with a hand over my forehead, I replied saying ‘you can just read it and find out’. Don’t worry, I thought to myself, I bottled up anything that might rock the boat and make us seasick inside myself. That must be why I keep feeling so nauseous.

you.
That last day we spent together embodied and intensified the rollercoaster of emotions I’d been through. When I spotted you at the bookstore we’d planned to meet at, you were picking out a pricey set of markers. ‘Your khiyon—a word in Shugni meaning the wife of a brother, used casually among friends—likes coloring’ was the simple explanation you offered. The whole affair was so you I didn’t know whether I wanted to laugh or to cry. I bought a comically large novel to tide me over for the month I’d be lazing around in the town we grew up in before starting my big-girl job. We went to meet my cousin, whom you’d formed a relationship with outside of me, at the money laundering cafe we’d hung out at before. Over halfway there I realized I’d left her wedding present at the bookstore, which was so me, and we had to double back to retrieve it. My cousin’s inquiries about the developments in your life were answered with quiet downplaying from you and animated boasting from me. You’ve said this to me many times and I don’t know if you understand just how mutual it is: I am so proud to know you.

After a while of me chatting with my cousin and taking periodic sips from your cup of too-sweet tea while you worked across the booth, we all set out on the long walk to my aunt’s house so my cousin and I could visit before I left the capital. In the quiet moments, my mind wandered back to the fantasies of domestic bliss I’d conjured up all those years ago. Except this time, with each of us now having jobs and bank accounts and rapidly developing frontal lobes, it all felt so much more… tangible. I had been planning to buy an apartment in an area of the city we both loved in a few years’ time, and now I imagined the two of us curled up on its living room couch. I thought of us spending summers just like this, sitting next to each other typing away by day and sitting across from each other at new restaurants by night. Each time I managed to look away, the aching desire for this proximity being dangled just out of reach in front of me washed over my body. I had to take a moment to bring myself back to giggling and jumping between conversations with you in Shugni and my cousin in Russian. I hugged you goodbye, whispering in your ear ‘I’ll miss you’ in a sudden spell of reckless defiance.

You unexpectedly texted me again later asking if I could come out for a bit. You were waiting at the park right near my parents’ apartment. This was our last meetup. Even if my flight got canceled and I stayed in the city tomorrow, I think we both knew we couldn’t see each other again. If we did, it felt as though something frightening and unpredictable was bound to happen. Our multiple strolls around the park so flat and large it seemed like there was barely anyone there, despite the many dozens of people milling about in the light-polluted night, were surprisingly short. We parted ways courteous and wholesome, each telling the other to keep in touch and promising we’ll always be there for one another. My walk back alone seemed to draw out endlessly.

In the time since, I’ve done nothing other to disturb the balance except asking you if you’ve seen the movie Past Lives. And all it took was give-or-take ten crash outs of varying degrees of intensity documented in varying forms of media, lengthy interventions from four different friends, and a constant onslaught of self-imposed distraction. Even now, I’m not quite sure if I’m in the clear yet. But the gods (my beautiful loving friends who pull the brakes hard when I’m about to make a rash and stupid decision… it’s like they care about me or something, so annoying I swear) have spoken, and they all say to leave that poor man alone.

One friend told me to imagine how I’d feel if I woke up tomorrow and you were married. I said that I could be happy for you, but only from a distance. Seeing how quickly I had been sucked into the idea of us, I am certain we couldn’t be friends if we saw each other any more frequently than once every couple of years. Another one told me that feeling this pain now is beautiful, and it’ll be beautiful to feel it again when I’m at your wedding, but then that’ll be it. I realized that whenever I imagined your wedding, I was always there single. A thought occurred to me: what if I got married before you? Why had I never considered that before? It clicked for me then that even though I could picture attending your wedding as a guest, I couldn’t picture myself getting married to someone that wasn’t you. Yet another friend illustrated very incisively how there was no possible good outcome for either of us in this scenario. If you feel the same, then I’d be throwing a wrench into your life as you are trying to figure things out with this girl and I’m about to move across the world in less than a month. If you don’t, I lose the longing of the boy who said he never wanted to be involved with anyone else when we broke up—and what have I become if even you don’t want me anymore? Of course, it’s incredibly selfish and wholly unfair to put the burden of my worth upon you, especially when it was I who ended things in the first place.

I know all of this and more. I’ve turned our muddy predicament over in my head so many times it’s become this smooth hikaru dorodango of an essay I present here. I’m trying my best now to sit with the uncertainty (or just in evasion of the turmoil that is sure to come if I do anything else). maybe i’m a coward, maybe i’m a saint, most likely i’m just a girl with too much time on her hands and more emotions than she knows how to deal with. all i know is that it’s hard to let you go when i can’t help but feel like we were torn up and rolled into the shape of souls from the same paper napkin.
